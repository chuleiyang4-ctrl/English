ENGLISH · LESSON 1.2

Spelling Patterns & Connected Speech

Words aren't spelled out loud — sound flows continuously. Learn the formulas that predict pronunciation from spelling, and the four habits that make native speech glide together.

Foundational · read this once, then let your ear absorb it through listening

I. From Letters to Sounds

English has only 26 letters for 44 sounds. The gap is closed by two things: syllable shape, and fixed two-or-three-letter "codes."

1. Vowel letters change with syllable shape

DailyAcademic●●

Closed syllable (ends in a consonant) → short vowel sound: cat /kæt/

Open syllable (ends with a silent e) → the vowel says its own name: make /meɪk/

Why is the e silent?

The final e doesn't disappear — it lends its energy to the vowel before it, stretching a short vowel into a long one, then goes quiet itself. That's why mad (closed, short /æ/) becomes made (open, long /eɪ/) with one letter added at the end.

2. Fixed consonant "codes"

DailyAcademic●

sh → /ʃ/ — fish, ship

ch → /tʃ/ — check, rich

th → /θ/ or /ð/ — think, this

tion → /ʃən/ — action, station

II. Four Laws of Connected Speech

Fluent speech isn't slower, careful pronunciation sped up — it's a different set of physical habits your mouth uses to save effort. This is usually why native speech is hard to follow even when every individual word is familiar.

Written as three separate words, spoken as one continuous sound. The consonant at the end of "Check" slides straight into the vowel of "it," and the /t/ in "it" softens into a quick tap before "out."

1. Consonant + vowel glide together

Daily●●

When one word ends in a consonant and the next starts with a vowel, they merge into a single beat.

Check it out → Check-i-tout /tʃekɪtaʊt/

2. Flap T — American speech's signature move

Daily●●●

When /t/ or /d/ sits between two vowels and isn't stressed, the tongue taps the gum ridge quickly — the result sounds close to a light /d/.

water → "wah-der" /wɑːtər/

get it → "ged-it"

3. H-dropping on pronouns

Daily●●

The /h/ in unstressed pronouns (he, him, her, his) often disappears in fast speech, and the previous word links straight to what's left.

Tell him → Tell-im /telɪm/

4. Function words collapse to schwa

Daily●●

Grammar words (for, to, and, at, of) lose their full vowel sound when unstressed and flatten to the neutral /ə/.

cup of tea → cup-a-tea /kʌp ə ti/

Practice

Say this sentence at a natural, fast pace: "Get out of it." Which two connected-speech laws from this lesson would a native speaker apply, and where?

Flap T: the /t/ in "Get" sits between two vowel sounds once linked to "out," so it flaps toward a light /d/ — "Ge-dout." Consonant + vowel linking: "out" ends in a consonant and "of" starts with a vowel, so they glide together — "ou-tof." Put together, a native speaker says something close to "Ge-dou-tofit", not four separate words.
