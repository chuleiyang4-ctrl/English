Where English Came From

English is a West Germanic language that absorbed huge amounts of French and Latin vocabulary through history. That single fact explains most of what feels "irregular" today.

One idea, layered wordsask (everyday) → question (formal) → interrogate (technical/legal). Same meaning, different register — a direct fossil of which conquering language a word arrived with.
Spelling froze, pronunciation moved onIn knight, the k was pronounced in medieval English. Speech dropped it centuries ago; the printing press locked the spelling in place before it could catch up.
Irregular verbs are historical leftoversgo → went isn't a logic failure — went was borrowed from a completely different old verb. Irregularity is usually history, not chaos.

How English Runs

Underneath the vocabulary, four operating rules generate almost every sentence you'll ever hear.

1

Sound is a finite set of contrasts. American English (GA) is the primary reference here; British English (RP) appears only as a labeled comparison where it differs.

2

Verbs drive the sentence. Every independent clause needs one core verb carrying the action, time, and mood.

3

Position reveals role. The same word means something different depending on where it sits — structure gives the clue, context gives the meaning.

4

Core first, details after. Always find "who did what" before layering on modifiers and detail.

What English Is Made Of

Six layers, each built on the one before it. Every course on this site sits on exactly one node of this graph.

Sound gives you the raw material for words. Vocabulary supplies meaning-bearing units. Syntax arranges them into sentences. Semantics is the meaning that arrangement produces. Context bends that meaning by situation. Discourse is meaning sustained across a whole text or conversation.
